What factors drive job satisfaction
in the Data Engineering Pilipinas Community?

Cross-tabs gave us dozens of significant findings. This analysis used four machine learning models and used SHAP to separate the real drivers from the noise. Here is what the models agreed on — and where they disagreed.

Problem: too many significant findings

The survey cross-tab compared three satisfaction groups: Low (1–4), Mid (5–7), and High (8–10), against every individual question in the survey. Almost every variable showed a statistically significant difference somewhere. Salary band. Work setup. Career stage. AI tool usage. Team size. Role type. Time in current salary. All of them flagged.

Cross-tabs answer "Is this variable related to satisfaction?" They do not answer "How much does it actually matter, relative to everything else?" With dozens of candidates all significant, there was no principled basis for deciding what to emphasize in the report.

The core problem
Significance is not importance. There should be a way to rank drivers by explanatory weight, not just detect their presence.

Solution: multi-model SHAP attribution

This analysis trained four models on the same dataset and applied SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) to each. SHAP assigns every feature a contribution score for each prediction, i.e., how much did this variable move the satisfaction score, on average, across all respondents?

A driver that shows high SHAP importance in a Random Forest and in a Lasso regression is an important driver. One that only appears in a linear model but not in the tree-based model is telling us something about the nature of the relationship (linear vs. non-linear) not just its existence.

The model divergence also gives insights

Insight · Remote work
sitework_WFH scores 0.62 in LR SHAP but only 0.09 in RF SHAP. This is because remote work has a clear linear relationship with satisfaction — the more remote, the higher the satisfaction, consistently. Tree-based models find more predictive power in non-linear feature interactions elsewhere and do not prioritize a simple linear trend. Running only RF would have buried this driver. Running only LR would have given it more importance vs. non-linear drivers.
Insight · AI use at work
A similar pattern appears for ai_work_Daily. High-satisfaction respondents are 20 percentage points more likely to use AI tools daily at work than low-satisfaction respondents (67.1% vs. 47.4%). The linear models detect this cleanly. The RF finds the relationship but prioritizes salary and career stage as stronger splits. This could be because AI daily use at work may be a proxy for workplace culture. Environments that encourage AI adoption may indirectly drive higher satisfaction, not the usage of AI per se.

Are the 8 drivers independent?

A correlation heatmap of the 8 key drivers shows no strong inter-correlations. They are pulling in different directions on satisfaction — not proxies for each other. This matters for our whole summary: each driver earned its place independently and can be discussed as a separate lever.
